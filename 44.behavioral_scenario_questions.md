# Behavioral / Scenario Questions — Asked by Engineering/Tech Managers

> These are asked by hiring/engineering managers (not tech-panel rounds) to assess judgment, ownership, communication, and how you operate under ambiguity or conflict — not raw technical depth. Each question now has **two answers** so you can pick whichever fits the conversation, or use the second as a fallback if you've already told the first story earlier in the interview. Each answer is broken into **S**ituation / **T**ask / **A**ction / **R**esult on separate lines purely so it's easy to scan while reading/rehearsing — **when spoken out loud, deliver it as one flowing answer, not as four labeled parts.** Kept to ~60–90 seconds total. Bracketed `[...]` spots are where you swap in your own specific project/incident — the surrounding structure and reasoning is reusable. Related: [[43.python_core_interview.md]], [[36.IQ.md]] (technical/system-design scenario answers).

## 1. Conflict & Disagreement

> Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate or your manager on a technical decision.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** In production, our RAG system's answer quality dropped. A teammate wanted to immediately bump retrieval top-k from 5 to 20 across all traffic to fix it fast. I wanted to first confirm top-k was actually the cause and roll it out gradually, not push an unverified change to everyone under incident pressure.
- **T:** Neither of us had hard proof yet — his read was "we know the symptom, just fix it now," mine was "we don't know the cause, a wrong guess at 100% traffic could make latency worse on top of the quality problem."
- **A:** Talked him into canarying it to 5% of traffic first instead of arguing about whose theory was right, and watched latency and answer relevance on that slice against the baseline.
- **R:** Top-k was in fact the issue — canary showed better relevance with latency still inside SLA, so we rolled it to 100%. Since then, any retrieval knob change in prod gets a canary step first, even during an incident when the instinct is to just push the fix everywhere.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** On the SDLC agent pipeline, a teammate wanted the code-scaffolding agent to keep retrying and patching its own output whenever tests failed, with a high retry cap, fully hands-off. I wanted it capped low and handed to a human after that, not left to keep "fixing" itself.
- **T:** His read was "let it iterate, that's the point of having an agent — more retries means less human time spent." Mine was "unbounded self-correction on its own output can drift from the original spec in ways nobody would notice until later, since it's grading its own work."
- **A:** Proposed a middle ground instead of arguing caps in the abstract — allow 2 retries, but log a diff between each retry's output and the original requirements each time, and route to human review if either the cap was hit or the diff looked like it had drifted from the spec, not just from the failing test.
- **R:** Caught a real case within the first week — the agent had "fixed" a failing test by loosening the assertion instead of fixing the actual bug. His original always-retry version wouldn't have caught that. Kept the hands-off behavior for the normal case, added a net for the case that actually mattered.

> Describe a conflict with a peer that became personal or tense. How did you resolve it?

**Answer 2**
- **S:** I was reviewing a teammate's PR and some of my comments landed harsher than I meant them to — he went quiet on messages for a couple of days after.
- **T:** Realized something was off and it needed fixing as a relationship thing, not just a code thing.
- **A:** Talked to him directly instead of over Slack, admitted the comments read colder than intended, and offered to walk through the actual disagreement on a call instead of trading review comments back and forth.
- **R:** We sorted the technical point in about ten minutes once it was a conversation. Since then I default to a quick call the moment a review thread starts feeling terse.

> Tell me about a time you had to push back on a manager's decision.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** My manager wanted a feature shipped on a tight timeline that skipped proper eval of an LLM classifier.
- **T:** Didn't want to just comply and also didn't want to flat-out refuse.
- **A:** Told him the actual risk in numbers — expected false-positive rate with no eval vs. with a one-day eval pass — and offered a smaller version that still hit the date but kept a basic guardrail in.
- **R:** He liked having the tradeoff spelled out instead of guessed at, and we shipped the scoped-down version on time.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** During a production incident, my manager wanted a hotfix pushed straight to prod with no review, just to close it out faster.
- **T:** I understood the urgency but didn't want to skip review on something touching order data.
- **A:** Offered a faster path instead of zero review — pulled in one other engineer for a focused ten-minute look at just the diff, not a full review cycle.
- **R:** The reviewer actually caught an edge case in the fix itself. We avoided a second incident and still shipped within the hour.

> How do you handle working with someone whose working style is very different from yours?

**Answer 1**
- **S:** Was paired with someone very detail-oriented while I tend to move fast and iterate.
- **T:** Had to work with him without it turning into rework on either side.
- **A:** Changed how I communicated instead of expecting him to adapt — started sharing a rough design doc before writing any code so he could weigh in early.
- **R:** Cut down rework a lot, and it was a cheap fix once I noticed the mismatch.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** A teammate wanted everything talked through live in meetings, I default to writing things down and working async.
- **T:** Needed a way to actually collaborate without either of us feeling unheard.
- **A:** Started sending a short written summary after every discussion we had. Gave him something to react to in the mode he preferred, and it gave me a record instead of relying on memory.
- **R:** Made our handoffs noticeably cleaner. Neither of us had to fully switch how we work, just adapt the interface between us.

## 2. Failure, Mistakes & Accountability

> Tell me about a time you made a significant mistake. What happened and what did you learn?

**Answer 1**  
- **S:** Built a caching layer for LLM responses and didn't scope the cache keys by tenant.
- **T:** A query from one tenant briefly served back another tenant's cached context. Needed to contain it fast.
- **A:** Noticed it within a couple hours from an odd eval metric spike, pulled the cache immediately, went through the affected requests, added tenant ID into the cache key, wrote a test specifically for cross-tenant isolation, and wrote up what happened for the team.
- **R:** Only a small number of requests were hit and nothing sensitive leaked. Now any shared-state change — caching, batching, pooling — gets a specific isolation check before merge, not after.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** Rolled out a retry mechanism for a matching job without a backoff cap. A downstream catalog API outage turned it into a retry storm.
- **T:** Needed to stop the storm, then figure out how the cap got missed in the first place.
- **A:** Killed the worker pool to stop hammering the API, added exponential backoff with a max retry count, then audited every other job in that service for the same gap.
- **R:** Found two more jobs missing the same cap before they caused their own incident. Now "what happens if this fails 100 times in a row" is a specific question I ask about any retry logic before merge.

> Describe a time a project you owned failed or missed its goal.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** Scoped a RAG redesign as a 2-week build, assuming a better embedding model would just fix retrieval quality.
- **T:** Turned out I couldn't actually verify quality without a proper golden-answer eval set, which wasn't in the plan.
- **A:** Built the eval harness once that became obvious. Took an extra week.
- **R:** Missed the deadline by about a week. Now eval-harness time is its own line item in any ML estimate I give, and I say upfront where I'm unsure instead of giving one confident number.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** Estimated adding tracing to the SDLC agent pipeline at two weeks, assuming I could bolt structured logging onto the existing agent code without touching its control flow.
- **T:** The agents didn't have clean step boundaries, so tracing meant restructuring how steps were called, not just adding log lines.
- **A:** Went back and refactored the orchestration into explicit steps first, which hadn't been part of the original plan.
- **R:** Took closer to a month. Now I check whether the thing I'm instrumenting actually has clean boundaries before I estimate instrumentation work as if it does.

> Tell me about a time you were wrong about a technical assumption and had to change course.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** Assumed hybrid search (BM25 + vector) would beat pure vector search on our corpus, going off general literature.
- **T:** Wanted to check that before committing to the extra complexity.
- **A:** Built both and ran an A/B on our actual documents instead of trusting the blog posts.
- **R:** Pure vector search was just as good and simpler — our corpus was mostly prose, not keyword-heavy. Dropped the hybrid approach. I hold opinions like that pretty loosely now until I've tested them on real data.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** Assumed a fuzzy string match on SKU/description would be good enough for the PO line-item matcher, based on how clean I thought the catalog data looked.
- **T:** Wanted to confirm that before building the confidence-scoring layer on top of it.
- **A:** Ran the matcher against a real batch of historical POs instead of the tidy test set I'd been developing against.
- **R:** Catalog data was messier than expected — abbreviated descriptions everywhere. Had to add a normalization step before matching could even run. Test against production-shaped data early, not fixtures you built yourself.

> How do you respond when you're blamed for something that wasn't (fully) your fault?

**Answer 1**
- **S:** Got blamed for something that wasn't entirely on me.
- **T:** Didn't want to deflect unfairly, but also didn't want to just eat blame that would hide the actual cause.
- **A:** Stuck to specifics — here's what I did, here's what happened after. Owned the part that actually was mine.
- **R:** The real cause got fixed instead of buried under a wrong explanation, and people trusted that I'd give it to them straight instead of getting defensive.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** A release from the SDLC pipeline shipped with a regression, and it initially got pinned on my eval pipeline for "not catching it."
- **T:** Wanted to clarify the actual gap without sounding like I was pushing blame onto someone else.
- **A:** Pulled up the eval run for that release and showed exactly what it did and didn't cover — the regression was in a code path the golden set didn't exercise yet.
- **R:** We added that path to the golden set instead of arguing about fault, and the pipeline's actual coverage got clearer to everyone, not just me.

## 3. Ownership & Leadership (without authority)

> Tell me about a time you took ownership of something outside your defined responsibilities.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** Our LLM costs had crept up over a quarter and nobody actually owned watching that.
- **T:** Noticed it in a billing review and decided to just deal with it even though it wasn't mine.
- **A:** Built a small dashboard breaking cost down by model/endpoint/team. Had no authority to mandate anything, so I proposed three levers — prompt caching, right-sizing the model, batching non-real-time jobs — with rough projected savings on each.
- **R:** Two of the three got adopted. Cut monthly spend by roughly a third.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** Noticed the review queue in the PO service was quietly growing, since we only tracked individual order SLAs, not queue depth as a whole.
- **T:** Wasn't my job to own ops health for that queue, but it was heading toward a real backlog.
- **A:** Added a queue-depth metric and a basic alert threshold, then flagged the trend to the team with a rough projection of when it'd become a problem.
- **R:** Got picked up before it turned into an incident, and queue depth became a metric we actually tracked going forward.

> Describe a situation where you had to lead a project without formal authority over the people involved.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** A cross-team migration to a new vector store, and I wasn't the tech lead on it.
- **T:** Someone had to coordinate it and nobody formally had that job.
- **A:** Kept a shared doc listing every dependency, let people volunteer for rough ownership instead of assigning it myself, ran a short weekly sync just to unblock things.
- **R:** Went smoothly. The influence came from having the clearest picture and doing the unglamorous coordination, not from a title.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** The eval pipeline needed quality criteria from three different agent owners, and there was no single lead assigned to pull that together.
- **T:** Without someone driving it, everyone would just keep scoring their own step differently.
- **A:** Set up short 1:1s with each owner to get their step's criteria, wrote a shared rubric doc from that, then circulated it for pushback before treating anything as final.
- **R:** Got alignment without anyone feeling like criteria had been imposed on them — it came from what they'd each said, not from a title deciding for them.

> Tell me about a time you had to make a decision with incomplete information and high stakes.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** A production RAG system started giving degraded answers and it wasn't obvious if it was the retriever, the reranker, or the LLM provider.
- **T:** Had to decide fast whether to roll back or keep digging, with users seeing it live.
- **A:** Rolled back right away to stop the bleeding, then dug into it afterward in a copy of the environment instead of debugging live.
- **R:** Turned out to be embedding-model version drift on the provider's side. Since then: if it's reversible and stakes are high, fix first, figure out why second.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** The PO matching engine started auto-approving orders at a noticeably higher rate than normal overnight, with nothing obviously broken in the logs.
- **T:** Had to decide within the hour whether to keep auto-approval live or shut it off, without knowing the cause yet.
- **A:** Turned off auto-approval as a precaution and routed everything to manual review, then traced it back — a pricing sync job had pushed stale data that made more orders score above threshold than should have.
- **R:** Fixed the sync job and turned auto-approval back on the same day. Same lesson as the RAG rollback: reduce blast radius first when you can't yet tell if something's a real signal or noise.

## 4. Prioritization, Deadlines & Pressure

> How do you prioritize when you have multiple competing deadlines?

**Answer 1**
- **S:** A customer-reported bug and a scheduled feature demo landed the same week.
- **T:** Needed to prioritize without just deciding unilaterally for everyone else affected.
- **A:** Ranked by impact and how reversible each was, flagged the conflict right away, said the bug should go first since it hit production users, and got explicit agreement to move the demo two days.
- **R:** Bug got fixed fast, demo moved with everyone's buy-in instead of quietly slipping.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** A flaky eval pipeline run and a partner demo that used that pipeline's output were both scheduled the same day.
- **T:** Unreliable numbers would make the demo look worse, but properly fixing the flakiness wasn't a same-day job.
- **A:** Didn't try to fix it fully in time. Ran the eval suite three times before the demo and used the median, and flagged to the team that flakiness itself was still an open item, not resolved.
- **R:** Demo ran fine on stable numbers, and the actual flakiness fix got scheduled right after instead of getting quietly forgotten once the pressure was off.

> Tell me about a time you had to deliver under a tight or unrealistic deadline.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** Got asked to ship an agentic workflow feature for a demo in a week — I'd have normally estimated two.
- **T:** Wanted to deliver something real without overselling what I could actually hit.
- **A:** Cut scope, not quality. Picked the smallest slice that showed the core value — a single-tool agent loop instead of full multi-agent orchestration — and was upfront about what was in and out.
- **R:** Delivered on time and it actually worked. I'd rather hand over something smaller that's solid than something bigger that's half-broken.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** Got asked to add a new pricing rule type to the matching engine before an end-of-quarter client commitment, originally scoped a sprint later.
- **T:** Wanted to hit it without cutting corners on something touching money.
- **A:** Narrowed it to the specific rule type the client actually needed instead of the general pricing-rule framework I'd been planning, and skipped the admin UI for it, wiring it directly into config for now.
- **R:** Shipped on time. Went back and built the proper UI the following sprint once the pressure was off.

> Describe a time you had to say no to a request or deprioritize something important to a stakeholder.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** A PM wanted a new retrieval feature added mid-sprint.
- **T:** Needed to protect committed work without just saying "no" and leaving it there.
- **A:** Explained what context-switching would cost, gave two real options — scope it properly for next sprint, or do a rough spike now if it genuinely couldn't wait.
- **R:** They picked next sprint once the tradeoff was actually laid out, no friction afterward.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** A client asked mid-project for the SDLC agent to also generate deployment runbooks, on top of requirements, scaffolding, and CI/CD it already produced.
- **T:** A fourth agent type wasn't a small addition and would've pushed the existing three out.
- **A:** Said no to doing it now, but scoped what it'd actually take separately and brought that to them as a follow-on phase instead of just declining.
- **R:** They accepted the phased plan, and the runbook agent became phase two once the first three had shipped and stabilized.

> How do you handle being overloaded with more work than you can realistically finish?

**Answer 1**
- **S:** Had more assigned than I could realistically get through.
- **T:** Needed to say something instead of quietly absorbing it.
- **A:** Listed everything, put a rough estimate on each, brought it to my manager as "here's what's committed, here's what's at risk, tell me what to prioritize or get me help."
- **R:** Turned invisible overload into an actual decision made with the full picture, instead of quiet overtime nobody knew about.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** Was carrying eval pipeline work, matcher bug fixes, and onboarding a new teammate, all in the same sprint.
- **T:** Realized partway through I wasn't going to do any of the three well at that pace.
- **A:** Told my manager directly which one I thought should slip, with reasoning, instead of trying to silently push through all three.
- **R:** Onboarding got reassigned to someone else for that sprint. The other two got done properly instead of three things done badly.

## 5. Ambiguity & Problem-Solving

> Tell me about a time you were given a vague or underspecified problem and had to figure out the right approach yourself.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** Got told to "make the RAG answers better." That's it, no more detail.
- **T:** Had to turn that into something actionable.
- **A:** Treated it as a diagnosis problem first — pulled a sample of bad responses and sorted failures into retrieval miss, generation hallucination, or ambiguous query.
- **R:** Retrieval miss was about 60% of it. That reframed the whole thing into a concrete plan — fix chunking, add a reranker — instead of me guessing at prompt tweaks that wouldn't have moved anything.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** Was told to "speed up the SDLC pipeline," no specifics on which step or by how much.
- **T:** Needed to turn that into something I could actually act on.
- **A:** Pulled trace data across recent runs and broke down wall-clock time per agent step instead of guessing which one felt slow.
- **R:** The scaffolding step was eating most of it, mostly from sequential file writes. Parallelized just that step instead of trying to speed up everything evenly.

> Describe a time you had to make a decision without a clear "right answer."

**Answer 1**
- **S:** Choosing between a fully-managed vector DB and self-hosting one — genuinely a tradeoff, no clean answer.
- **T:** Team needed to decide anyway.
- **A:** Wrote up the tradeoffs plainly — cost now and at 10x scale, how much ops bandwidth we actually had, vendor lock-in risk — and made a recommendation.
- **R:** Team decided with the tradeoffs actually visible instead of the choice looking obvious when it wasn't.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** Deciding whether the PO matcher should hard-fail on missing catalog data, or fall back to a lower-confidence score and route to manual review.
- **T:** Both options had real downsides, no obviously correct choice.
- **A:** Looked at how often the "missing data" case actually occurred historically, and weighed that against what it'd cost in review queue volume versus orders silently failing.
- **R:** Went with fallback to manual review — missing data was rare enough not to flood the queue but frequent enough that hard-failing meant real orders just disappearing.

> Tell me about a time you had to learn something completely new quickly to solve a problem.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** Team started adopting agentic workflows and I had zero production experience with multi-agent orchestration.
- **T:** Needed to get useful fast, no long ramp-up available.
- **A:** Read the core framework docs, then built a throwaway toy agent over a weekend just to find the sharp edges myself.
- **R:** That hands-on mess-around was more useful than reading would've been on its own, and I applied it straight to the real use case.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** Needed to get up to speed on Neo4j fast once we decided the SDLC pipeline needed a dependency graph for module context.
- **T:** Had never touched a graph database before that project.
- **A:** Modeled a small real slice of our actual module dependencies first instead of working through generic tutorials, so I hit the specific query patterns I'd actually need.
- **R:** Had a working schema in a couple of days, rough edges and all, and iterated it from there once it was wired into the real pipeline.

## 6. Communication & Stakeholder Management

> Tell me about a time you had to explain a complex technical concept to a non-technical stakeholder.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** Had to explain to a product stakeholder why an LLM feature couldn't be 100% accurate.
- **T:** Wanted the conversation to actually go somewhere, not just reassure him.
- **A:** Skipped words like "hallucination rate" and used an analogy — it's like a very well-read intern, usually right, occasionally confidently wrong, so anything high-stakes needs a review step.
- **R:** Conversation shifted from "why isn't this perfect" to "where do we actually need a human checking it," which was the real question.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** Had to explain to a client why the PO matching confidence score wasn't the same as "percent correct."
- **T:** He kept reading a 90% score as "90% chance this specific order is right," which wasn't really what it meant.
- **A:** Reframed it around what the score was actually built from — how closely line items matched catalog data and pricing — instead of treating it as a black-box probability, and walked through a couple of real orders at different scores.
- **R:** He stopped treating the number as one single truth and started asking about the components behind it, which was the more useful conversation to have.

> How do you communicate a technical tradeoff to a non-technical stakeholder? (client-facing, consulting context)

**Answer 1**
- **S:** On the PO→SO automation project, the client wanted every purchase order auto-approved with zero manual review, just to go faster.
- **T:** Had to explain why a confidence threshold, not full automation, was the right call — without drowning him in ML terms.
- **A:** Framed it as a dial, not a switch: any automated system is trading speed for occasional errors, the threshold controls that trade, it doesn't remove it. Showed two scenarios side by side — threshold at 95% vs. 80% — in plain terms: orders auto-approved per day vs. roughly how many error orders would slip through to their ERP. Let him pick the risk level instead of me deciding it for him.
- **R:** He picked a threshold knowing what he was trading off, and the conversation moved to "who reviews the below-threshold queue" instead of "why isn't this fully automatic." On a consulting project specifically, the point isn't to make the client feel good in the room, it's making sure whatever they sign off on is the tradeoff they actually understood.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** A product stakeholder wanted the SDLC agent's code scaffolding step to always produce fully working code, no manual cleanup ever.
- **T:** Had to explain that "always correct" wasn't realistic for generated code without ballooning scope to get there.
- **A:** Framed it as effort saved rather than effort eliminated — showed how much of a typical module the agent already got right versus what still needed a human pass, using real recent runs as examples.
- **R:** Expectation shifted from "replace the step entirely" to "cut the time this step takes," which was actually achievable and became what we optimized for afterward.

> Describe a time you had to deliver bad news to a stakeholder or manager.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** Found out a committed feature would miss its date because a third-party API's rate limits were way lower than what was documented.
- **T:** Wanted to flag it before it became a surprise to anyone.
- **A:** Said something the moment I confirmed it — the cause, the new estimate, and two options: ask the vendor for a higher rate limit, or degrade gracefully with local caching in the meantime.
- **R:** People took it fine because it came early with options attached, not late as a surprise.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** Found out partway through a sprint that the eval golden set had been silently stale for weeks — someone had changed the underlying data format and nothing flagged it.
- **T:** Meant recent eval scores couldn't really be trusted, right before a release review, which is not a great time to find that out.
- **A:** Told my manager as soon as I confirmed it, how far back it went and what it meant for the upcoming release decision — not just "there's a bug somewhere."
- **R:** Release got held a few days to regenerate the golden set properly instead of shipping on numbers nobody could actually vouch for.

> Tell me about a time you had to manage expectations with a client or stakeholder who wanted something unrealistic.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** A stakeholder wanted sub-second responses out of a multi-step agentic pipeline making several LLM calls.
- **T:** Had to say no to the literal ask without it sounding like an excuse.
- **A:** Broke down where the latency actually came from — sequential LLM calls, not orchestration overhead — and showed what was doable with parallelization and a smaller routing model, versus what would actually require changing the requirement, like streaming partial results instead.
- **R:** The "no" landed as expertise instead of excuse-making, and we agreed on the version that was actually achievable.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** A stakeholder wanted the PO matcher to hit near-100% auto-approval and zero mismatches at the same time, treating them as independent goals.
- **T:** Needed to explain they trade against each other and you can't max both at once.
- **A:** Plotted automation rate against error rate across a range of thresholds using real historical data, and let him look at the actual curve instead of me just asserting a tradeoff existed.
- **R:** He picked a point on the curve himself once it was visible, and stopped asking for "both, maxed."

## 7. Feedback — Giving & Receiving

> Tell me about a time you received critical feedback. How did you respond?

**Answer 1**
- **S:** A senior engineer told me my PRs were technically fine but too big to review properly, and it was slowing the team down.
- **T:** Wanted to respond well instead of getting defensive about it.
- **A:** Sat with it for a bit, admitted the pattern was real, started splitting work into smaller PRs that could actually be reviewed independently. Checked back in with him a few weeks later.
- **R:** He confirmed it had actually changed, so it wasn't just me saying I'd fix it.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** A lead told me I tended to over-engineer early versions of things, building for scale a feature didn't need yet.
- **T:** Wanted to actually check if that was fair instead of just agreeing on the spot to be agreeable.
- **A:** Went back and looked at a couple of my recent PRs with that lens. Honestly, some of it held up. Started explicitly asking "does this need to handle 10x load today" before adding abstraction.
- **R:** My next few PRs were noticeably smaller, and review comments about scope dropped off a lot.

> Describe a time you had to give difficult feedback to a peer or someone you worked with.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** A teammate kept breaking the shared staging environment because he wasn't running tests before pushing.
- **T:** Needed to say something without it landing as an attack.
- **A:** Brought it up privately and specifically — three breaks this month, all traced to skipped tests, can we make running them part of the pre-push habit — and offered to pair on a pre-push hook to automate it.
- **R:** Landed fine because it was specific and came with a fix, not just a complaint.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** A teammate's PRs kept bundling unrelated refactors in with the actual fix, which made them slow and confusing to review.
- **T:** Needed to say something before it became a habit that was hard to walk back.
- **A:** Brought it up after a specific PR, pointed at the exact lines that were unrelated cleanup, and asked if he'd split those out going forward — framed around review speed, not style preference.
- **R:** He agreed once it was framed that way, and it mostly stopped after that one conversation.

> How do you handle feedback you disagree with?

**Answer 1**
- **S:** Got feedback I didn't agree with at first.
- **T:** Wanted to respond well without either caving or brushing it off.
- **A:** Asked clarifying questions first to make sure I actually understood the concern. Still disagreed after that, said so with my reasoning, but implemented the parts I didn't have solid pushback on.
- **R:** Keeps me from being either a pushover or someone who can't take feedback.

**Answer 2**
- **S:** Was told my documentation on the eval pipeline was "too detailed" and should be trimmed down.
- **T:** Disagreed at first, since that detail had already saved someone a debugging session earlier that month.
- **A:** Asked what specifically felt like too much. Turned out it was more a structure problem than a content one — everything was on one long page instead of split by audience.
- **R:** Split it into a quick-start section and a deep-reference section instead of cutting content. Both the feedback and my instinct turned out partly right.

## 8. Teamwork & Collaboration

> Tell me about a time you had to work closely with someone you didn't naturally get along with.

**Answer 1**
- **S:** Had to work closely with someone I just didn't click with personally.
- **T:** Needed to keep that from leaking into the actual work.
- **A:** Kept it strictly professional — clear communication about the task, and I called out his good ideas publicly when he had them.
- **R:** Never became close, but it stayed functional and the project shipped on time.

**Answer 2**

## 13. Quick-Fire Rapid Rounds

Short prompts managers sometimes fire in rapid succession — keep answers to 2-3 sentences each; too short to need STAR breakdown. Each has a second option in case you've used the first recently.

- **Describe yourself in three words.** → Pick three you can back with a 10-second example each (e.g., "pragmatic, direct, curious"). Alt set: "grounded, curious, thorough" — works if the interview's leaned more technical.
- **What motivates you at work?** → Concrete outcomes I can measure, and having ownership over decisions instead of just executing someone else's. Alt: solving something nobody's cracked yet, where you don't know the right approach going in.
- **How do you handle stress?** → Break it into the smallest next actionable step instead of holding the whole problem in my head at once. Alt: step away for ten minutes if I can — coming back with a clear head beats pushing through foggy.
- **What's a decision you'd make differently in hindsight?** → Reuse a trimmed version of a §2 failure story. Alt: the hybrid-search assumption from §2, told with more focus on "I'd test earlier next time" as the hindsight point.
- **How do you handle repetitive or "boring" work?** → Look for what should be automated — repetitive work is usually pointing at a script that hasn't been written yet. Alt: reasonable repetition is fine short-term; it's when it's been the same manual step for months that it's actually worth stopping to fix.
- **What do you do when you don't know the answer to something in a meeting?** → Say so directly, give a specific time I'll follow up by, and actually follow up. Guessing out loud costs more trust than admitting a gap does. Alt: if it's something I can check in under a minute, I'll just say "let me check" and look it up live instead of guessing or stalling.
- **Tell me about a time you changed your mind about something you were previously confident about.** → Reuse the hybrid-search-assumption story from §2, or the junior-teammate story from §9.
- **What's something you do that most engineers don't?** → Tie back to a concrete habit already established elsewhere (e.g., budgeting eval time as a first-class estimate line item, from §2/§4).

## 14. Notes on Delivery

- The **S/T/A/R** split above is a **reading/rehearsal aid only** — say it out loud as one continuous story, never as four labeled chunks ("Situation is... Task was...").
- Keep it proportioned: **S+T ≈ 2 sentences, A ≈ 3-4 sentences, R ≈ 1-2 sentences** — managers lose the thread past ~90 seconds.
- Always end on **R** — a result, not just the action — "and what changed because of it" is what separates a story from a status update.
- Reuse stories across categories on purpose (a single strong incident often answers "failure," "ownership," and "pressure" questions) — don't force a different anecdote for every question just because the wording differs.
- Have 4-5 core stories memorized cold (one clear failure, one clear conflict, one clear ownership-without-authority, one clear ambiguity/problem-solving) and map every question above onto the nearest one rather than inventing new material live.
- With two answers per question now, don't feel obligated to memorize both — treat Answer 2 as a backup for when you've already told Answer 1 earlier in the same interview, or when the interviewer's follow-up makes it obviously the better fit.
- Let a few answers stay a little uneven or informal when you say them out loud — real recall isn't perfectly polished, and sounding too rehearsed reads as less credible than a slightly rough but specific answer.
